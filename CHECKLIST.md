# Before you make it public: a checklist

Publishing is the one step you cannot fully take back. Anything public can be copied or saved within minutes, even if you delete it later. Use this list before you press the button. Each line is a yes-or-no question. If the answer is "no" or "I'm not sure," stop and fix it first.

Examples come from this repository, Roll and Read, a small phone spelling game.

## 1. Before you start

- [ ] **Do I know exactly what I am making public?** Write the list of files. Publish a deliberate copy, not your whole working folder.
- [ ] **Is it all mine, or do I have the right to share it?** Text, images, sounds, fonts, code, worksheets, and ideas from other people each need permission or a license that allows sharing.
- [ ] **Is anyone else's work or information in it?** Classmates' work, names, emails, photos, grades. If yes, get their permission or remove it.
- [ ] **Does my instructor, employer, or school have a rule about this work?** Ask before you publish graded or course material.

## 2. Clean it

- [ ] **No secrets.** Search for passwords, keys, tokens, and login files. If one ever reached a public place, change it first, then clean up.
- [ ] **No private details of mine.** Check for your home folder path, computer name, phone number, address, and photo location data.
- [ ] **No scratch files.** Remove test data, old drafts, notes to self, downloaded copies of other people's material.
- [ ] **Look at the history, not just the files.** A secret you deleted last week is still in the history. Start from a clean copy if you are unsure.

## 3. Explain it

- [ ] **The first lines of the README say what it is, who it is for, and how to try it.** Example: "Open the link on a phone and play. Nothing to install, no account."
- [ ] **The README says what it does with data**, even if the answer is "nothing is collected."
- [ ] **The README lists what is unfinished, untested, or unreviewed.** Naming a gap is a strength.
- [ ] **A "how this was made" note says which tools I used, including AI, what I asked them to do, and what I checked myself.**
- [ ] **I did not claim more than I checked.** Words like "secure," "accurate," "private," and "approved" need evidence.

## 4. Credit and license

- [ ] **A `LICENSE` file is in the top folder, and the README names it.** With no license, other people have no right to reuse your work.
- [ ] **I only license what is mine.** Borrowed material keeps its own license. Say so.
- [ ] **A credits section lists everything I did not make:** what it is, where it came from, and the license it came with.
- [ ] **My name or handle is on it the way I want it shown.** Check the author name on your commits too.

## 5. Test it as a stranger

- [ ] **Open it in a private window**, not logged in to anything.
- [ ] **Open it on a phone** if it is meant for phones.
- [ ] **Click every link.** None may lead to a private page, a login wall, or a path on your own computer.
- [ ] **Follow the README steps exactly as written** on a machine that is not yours, or ask a friend to.
- [ ] **Ask one person who has not seen it to read the README and tell you what it is.** If they are wrong, fix the README.

## 6. Publish

- [ ] **Work on a branch and get a second pair of eyes on the change** before it goes live.
- [ ] **Check what you are about to push.** Read the list of changed files one last time.
- [ ] **Publish.** Then repeat the stranger test on the live version.

## 7. After

- [ ] **Say whether the project is finished, paused, or open to suggestions,** and how to reach you.
- [ ] **Know how to take it down** (make the repository private, or turn off the page) if you find a problem.
- [ ] **If you find a secret or private detail, change the secret, remove the detail, and tell your instructor.** Do not try to hide that it happened.

### Choosing a license (short guide)

Pick one on purpose, and say which one. If you are unsure, ask your instructor. This is guidance for coursework, not legal advice.

| What you made | A common choice | What it means in one line |
|---|---|---|
| Code | MIT or BSD 2-Clause | Anyone may use and change it, if they keep your notice. They are very close to each other |
| Writing, images, handouts, art | Creative Commons CC BY 4.0 | Anyone may share and adapt it if they credit you |
| Anything you want to keep control of | No license, with a clear note | Others may look but may not reuse it |

A license covers only what you own. It does not cover a worksheet, picture, or text you copied from somewhere else.

Keep this list with your project. When you find something it missed, tell your instructor so the list gets better.
